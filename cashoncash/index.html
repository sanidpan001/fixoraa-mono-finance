Cash-on-Cash: The Number Your Bank Account Feels

Cap rate judges the property. Cash-on-cash return judges your deal. It answers the question every investor actually cares about at the kitchen table: I am wiring $73,000 out of my savings — how much cash lands back in my account each year? Divide year-one cash flow by total cash invested and you have it. In the example above, $2,965 of annual cash flow on $73,000 invested is a 4.06% cash-on-cash return. Simple, personal, and impossible to fudge once both halves are counted honestly.

The metric's honesty comes from its denominator. Cap rate divides by the full purchase price, as if you paid cash. Cash-on-cash divides by the cash you actually deployed — down payment, closing costs, renovation, reserves. That makes it the right tool for comparing leveraged deals against each other and against non-real-estate alternatives like bonds or dividend stocks, where the "cash invested" concept is identical.

Counting True Cash Invested

The most common cash-on-cash error is a light denominator. Investors divide by the down payment and stop, which flatters every deal. The calculator above forces the full accounting: down payment (your equity on day one), closing costs (lender fees, title, escrow — typically 2 to 5 percent of price), renovation (the rehab budget you will actually spend before or just after closing), and reserves (the vacancy-and-repair cushion smart investors set aside). Skip any of these and your return is a fantasy — the money left your account whether or not it appears in the formula.

Two judgment calls deserve attention. First, reserves: counting three to six months of expenses as "invested" lowers your stated return, but that cash is genuinely committed to the deal and unavailable for other investments. Second, sweat equity: if you do the rehab yourself, the honest denominator includes the market value of that labor or at least the materials — otherwise you are paying yourself an invisible wage and calling it return.

Building Year-One Cash Flow

The numerator is net operating income minus debt service. Start with gross rent, subtract vacancy (5 to 10 percent, or trailing actuals), subtract the full operating expense stack — taxes, insurance, maintenance, management, HOA, utilities you cover — and then subtract the annual mortgage payment (principal and interest). What remains is the cash the property throws off after everyone else is paid. In the worked example: $24,000 rent, minus $1,200 vacancy, minus $7,320 expenses gives $15,480 NOI; minus $12,515 of annual debt service leaves $2,965.

Notice what is not subtracted: income taxes and depreciation. Cash-on-cash is a pre-tax metric by convention — your tax bracket changes the after-tax number, and depreciation shields vary by owner. Also not subtracted: the principal portion of your mortgage is not an "expense" in the economic sense (it builds your equity), but it is cash out the door, so it stays in the payment for cash-flow purposes. This is a cash metric, not an accounting metric.

Leverage: The Amplifier in Both Directions

Here is the relationship that governs every financed deal: when your interest rate is below the cap rate, leverage amplifies your cash-on-cash return above the cap rate (positive leverage); when rates exceed the cap rate, leverage drags it below (negative leverage). The example shows the happy case: a 7.04% cap rate financed at 6.5% produces a 4.06% cash-on-cash — wait, that is below the cap rate. Why?

Because positive leverage needs the rate below the cap rate and enough spread to overcome the math of the down payment. At 6.5% versus 7.04%, the spread is thin, and the debt service still consumes most of the NOI — the return lands below the unleveraged yield. Widen the spread (cheaper debt, higher cap) and cash-on-cash climbs past the cap rate; narrow it and leverage becomes a headwind. In high-rate environments, many 5%-cap deals show negative cash flow — the property is sound, the financing is expensive. The calculator's side-by-side dials make this visible instantly: if the teal number trails the white one by a lot, your loan is eating your lunch.

What Is a Good Cash-on-Cash Return?

Targets vary by strategy. Cash-flow investors in Midwest and Southern markets often demand 10 to 12 percent or they walk. Balanced investors accept 7 to 9 percent with some appreciation expected. Appreciation-market buyers on the coasts may accept 4 to 6 percent — or even break-even — betting that rent growth and price gains carry the total return. There is no universal hurdle; there is only your hurdle, set against your alternatives. A useful gut check: if your cash-on-cash return is below the yield on a boring bond fund with similar risk, the property needs a convincing growth story to justify itself.

Compare consistently. Cash-on-cash is a year-one metric — do not compare your stabilized year-three projection against someone else's year-one actual. And when a seller quotes a dazzling cash-on-cash figure, ask which denominator they used. Nine times out of ten, the closing costs and rehab mysteriously vanished.

The All-Cash Edge Case

Buy with no mortgage and something elegant happens: cash flow equals NOI, cash invested equals price plus closing costs, and cash-on-cash converges to the cap rate (slightly below it, since closing costs inflate the denominator). The calculator handles this naturally — set the down payment to 100% and the loan math drops to zero. All-cash deals are the cleanest comparison point between markets because financing differences vanish entirely.

Mistakes That Inflate the Number

Watch for five classic distortions. Missing expenses — no management fee, no maintenance reserve, fantasy vacancy — inflate the numerator. Light denominators — forgetting closing costs and rehab — shrink the base. Pro forma rents the property never achieved inflate year one. Interest-only math quoted as if it were a full payment understates debt service. And ignoring the refinance or rate reset — that 5% ARM becomes 8% in year three, and your cash-on-cash goes with it. Run the calculator with trailing actuals and today's real rate first; optimism gets its own scenario second.

Beyond Year One: What Cash-on-Cash Misses

Cash-on-cash is a snapshot, and wealth is a movie. It ignores rent growth (your return should improve yearly as rents rise against fixed debt), principal paydown (every payment quietly buys you more of the building), appreciation, and depreciation tax shields (paper losses that shelter real income). A 5% cash-on-cash deal with 3% annual rent growth and normal amortization often delivers a mid-teens internal rate of return over a decade — the year-one number was just the trailer.

Use cash-on-cash for what it is brilliant at: screening deals and comparing financing structures side by side. Should you put 20% or 30% down? Run both and watch the dials. Fifteen-year or thirty-year loan? The payment difference shows up in the numerator immediately. Then, for the final decision, graduate to a multi-year IRR that captures growth, paydown, taxes, and sale. The year-one cash yield gets you in the door; the IRR tells you whether to stay.

Refinancing Resets the Math

Cash-on-cash is not frozen at purchase — refinancing rewrites it. Take the worked example: a $165,000 loan at 7% costs about $1,098 a month. Refinance to 5.5% and the payment drops to roughly $937 — about $161 a month, or $1,930 a year, straight into cash flow. The return jumps from 4.06% to roughly 6.7% on the same $73,000 invested, with the property itself unchanged. This is why experienced investors say they buy for the property and refinance for the return: the asset's yield was always there, the financing was just temporarily expensive.

The honest way to model a refinance: keep the original cash invested in the denominator (plus refinance closing costs, typically $2,000–$4,000), and use the new payment in the numerator. If you pull cash out in the refinance, the math gets interesting — your invested cash drops while cash flow stays similar, which can send cash-on-cash soaring into the teens. That "infinite return" feeling is real but not free: you traded equity for it, and the higher loan balance raises your risk if rents dip. Run both scenarios above before you sign.

Frequently asked questions

What is cash-on-cash return?

Your annual pre-tax cash flow divided by the total cash you invested, as a percentage. Cash flow is rent minus operating expenses and mortgage payments; cash invested is down payment plus closing costs, rehab and reserves.

What is a good cash-on-cash return?

Many investors target 8–12%, but it depends on market and strategy. Cash-flow markets often demand 10%+, while coastal appreciation markets may accept 4–6%. Set your hurdle against your alternatives, like bond yields.

How is cash-on-cash different from cap rate?

Cap rate ignores financing (NOI ÷ price, as if all cash). Cash-on-cash includes your mortgage payment and divides by only your out-of-pocket cash. When loan rates sit below the cap rate, leverage pushes cash-on-cash above the cap rate.

Do I include closing costs in cash invested?

Yes — every dollar out of pocket counts: down payment, lender and title fees, escrow prepaids, renovation and reserves. Leaving them out is the most common way the return gets overstated.

Can cash-on-cash return be negative?

Yes. When interest rates exceed the cap rate, debt service can exceed NOI and you feed the property monthly. Know the exact monthly shortfall before you commit — some investors accept it for appreciation, but never by accident.

Does cash-on-cash include appreciation or tax benefits?

No. It is strictly the year-one cash yield. Appreciation, principal paydown and depreciation shields are real returns that belong in a multi-year IRR analysis.
