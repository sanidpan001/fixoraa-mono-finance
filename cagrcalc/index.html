What CAGR Actually Measures

CAGR — the Compound Annual Growth Rate — answers one question: at what steady yearly rate would an investment have had to grow to go from its start value to its end value? If ₹10,000 becomes ₹20,000 in 5 years, the CAGR is 14.87%. That does not mean the investment grew 14.87% every year; real returns bounce around. CAGR smooths the journey into a single comparable number, which is exactly why fund fact sheets, stock screeners and pitch decks all quote it.

The formula is CAGR = (End Value ÷ Start Value)^(1 ÷ Years) − 1. The exponent is the whole trick: it annualises total growth, so a 100% gain over 5 years (14.87%) is correctly shown as weaker than a 100% gain over 3 years (25.99%). Simple average returns cannot do this — they ignore compounding and routinely overstate performance.

Why CAGR Beats "Average Return"

Imagine an investment that gains 50% in year one and loses 50% in year two. The simple average return is 0%, which sounds like you broke even. In reality ₹100 became ₹150, then ₹75 — you lost a quarter of your money, and the CAGR is −13.4%. This is volatility drag: losses hurt more than equal-sized gains help, and only the geometric compounding in CAGR captures it. Whenever someone quotes an arithmetic average return, mentally discount it — CAGR is the honest number.

CAGR vs absolute return vs XIRR

Absolute return ("my money doubled") ignores time; CAGR adds the time dimension. XIRR goes further and handles irregular cash flows — multiple deposits and withdrawals at different dates — which CAGR cannot, since CAGR assumes a single lump sum at the start and a single value at the end. Use CAGR for lump-sum comparisons, XIRR for SIPs and messy real-world portfolios.

Reading CAGR Like a Professional

A CAGR number is only meaningful with its time period attached. "15% CAGR" over 3 years during a bull run says far less than "15% CAGR" over 15 years through two crashes. Professionals always ask for the period, the benchmark's CAGR over the same period, and the volatility behind the number. A 14% CAGR with gut-wrenching 40% drawdowns is a worse lived experience than a 12% CAGR that never dropped more than 10%.

Also watch the endpoints. CAGR is hypersensitive to start and end dates: measuring from a market bottom flatters the number, measuring from a peak punishes it. This is why cherry-picked "since inception" CAGRs in marketing material deserve scepticism — nudge either endpoint and the story changes.

The Reverse Question: What CAGR Do You Need?

More useful than measuring the past is planning the future: what CAGR do you need to turn today's corpus into tomorrow's goal? The reverse formula is Required End Value = Start × (1 + CAGR)^Years, or flipped, Required CAGR = (Goal ÷ Start)^(1 ÷ Years) − 1. If you have ₹5,00,000 and need ₹20,00,000 in 10 years for a house down payment, you need a 14.87% CAGR — a number that immediately tells you the goal demands aggressive equity exposure, not fixed deposits.

This reverse mode is where financial plans live or die. A required CAGR above 15% over a decade is historically equity-like and uncertain; above 20% is speculative. If the maths demands 25%, the honest response is to raise the monthly investment or extend the timeline — not to chase riskier assets and hope.

Benchmark CAGRs for context

  • Indian large-cap equity indices have delivered roughly 11–13% CAGR over long 10–15 year stretches, with wide variation by period.
  • Fixed deposits and PPF sit near 7–7.5% — barely above long-run inflation of 5–6%.
  • Gold in rupee terms has compounded around 8–10% over multi-decade periods.
  • A 10% CAGR doubles money in about 7.2 years; 15% doubles it in under 5.

Comparing Two Investments Fairly

Side-by-side CAGR comparison only works when the periods match. Comparing a fund's 5-year CAGR with another's 3-year CAGR is meaningless — different markets, different luck. Align the dates, compare against the benchmark for the same window, and then look at consistency: rolling 3-year CAGRs over a decade reveal whether outperformance was skill or one lucky year. The comparison step in the calculator enforces this discipline by placing both investments on the same timeline.

The Year-by-Year Table: Seeing Compounding

A growth table that applies the CAGR each year makes compounding visceral: the absolute rupee gains accelerate because each year's growth builds on a bigger base. On ₹10,000 at 14.87%, year one adds ₹1,487 but year five adds ₹2,582 — the same rate, nearly double the rupees. This accelerating curve is why starting early dominates every other variable in investing; time in the market does the heavy lifting that higher returns cannot reliably replicate.

Common CAGR Mistakes

  • Comparing CAGRs over different time periods.
  • Ignoring that CAGR hides volatility — two identical CAGRs can feel completely different.
  • Projecting a short, lucky CAGR decades into the future.
  • Forgetting inflation: a 12% nominal CAGR at 6% inflation is only ~5.7% real.
  • Using CAGR for SIPs or irregular cash flows — that needs XIRR.

Using CAGR to Choose Funds

When comparing mutual funds, always compare CAGR over identical periods — ideally rolling 3-year and 5-year windows, not cherry-picked point-to-point stretches. A fund that outperformed over the last 5 years but lagged on rolling 3-year CAGRs may have had one lucky year. Also compare against the benchmark's CAGR for the same window: a 14% CAGR means little if the index did 15%. Finally, prefer funds whose CAGR is consistent across market cycles over those with a higher but erratic number — consistency compounds better in real portfolios because investors actually stay invested.

The Maths Behind the Formula

CAGR is the geometric mean of annual growth factors. If yearly returns are r1, r2, …, rn, the total growth factor is (1+r1)(1+r2)…(1+rn), and CAGR is that product raised to 1/n, minus 1. This is why volatility drag exists: by the AM-GM inequality, the geometric mean is always below the arithmetic mean when returns vary. A quick sanity check — the Rule of 72 — falls out of the same maths: ln(2)/ln(1+r) is close to 72/(100r) for typical rates. Understanding that CAGR is a geometric mean tells you exactly when to trust it (lump sums, full periods) and when not to (SIPs, partial data).

Real vs Nominal CAGR: The Inflation Cut

A 12% nominal CAGR sounds excellent until you subtract inflation. The exact conversion is (1+nominal)/(1+inflation)−1: at 6% inflation, 12% nominal is only 5.66% real. Over long periods this cut is brutal — it halves the apparent growth. Always state which one you mean: fund marketing quotes nominal, but your purchasing power compounds at the real rate. When comparing investments across countries or eras with different inflation, real CAGR is the only honest metric. As a rule of thumb, subtract roughly the inflation rate for quick estimates, but use the division formula when precision matters.

Frequently asked questions

How is CAGR calculated?

CAGR = (End Value ÷ Start Value)^(1 ÷ Years) − 1, expressed as a percentage. For example, ₹10,000 growing to ₹20,000 in 5 years gives (2)^(0.2) − 1 = 14.87%.

What is the difference between CAGR and absolute return?

CAGR smooths growth into one steady yearly rate, while absolute return just states total growth without time. A 100% absolute return over 5 years (14.87% CAGR) is very different from 100% over 2 years (41.42% CAGR).

Can I use CAGR for SIP returns?

No. CAGR assumes a single lump sum invested at the start. For SIPs or irregular deposits and withdrawals, use XIRR, which accounts for the timing of each cash flow.

What does a negative CAGR mean?

A negative CAGR means the investment lost value overall — the end value is below the start value. For instance, ₹10,000 falling to ₹8,000 over 3 years is a −7.17% CAGR.

What is a good CAGR?

It depends on the asset and period, but as a rough guide: 7–8% for safe fixed income, 11–13% for long-term Indian equity, and anything above 15% sustained over a decade is exceptional.

How do I find the CAGR needed to reach a goal?

Yes — that is the reverse CAGR question. Required CAGR = (Goal ÷ Current)^(1 ÷ Years) − 1. If the required rate looks unrealistically high, increase your investment amount or extend the timeline instead of chasing risk.
