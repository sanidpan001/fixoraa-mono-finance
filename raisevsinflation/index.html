      Nominal vs Real: The Raise You Got vs the Raise You Kept

      When your salary goes from $80,000 to $86,400, the payslip says you got an 8% raise. But if the general price level rose 5% over the same period, your money buys less than it used to — so the real raise, the change in your purchasing power, is smaller than 8%. Economists call the payslip number the nominal raise and the purchasing-power number the real raise. Confusing the two is one of the most common money misunderstandings there is, and this calculator exists to make the distinction visible with the arithmetic shown in full.

      The intuition is straightforward. A raise increases the number of dollars you earn; inflation increases the number of dollars things cost. Your standard of living depends on the ratio between the two. If both rise at the same rate, you can buy exactly what you could before — your real raise is zero, even though the nominal number moved. If prices rise faster than your pay, your real raise is negative: you earn more dollars and can afford less.

      The Formula, Step by Step

      The real raise is computed with this exact expression, where n is the nominal raise percent and i is the inflation percent:

      real raise = ((1 + n/100) / (1 + i/100) − 1) × 100

      Walk through it with n = 8 and i = 5, the defaults in the calculator above:

      1. Convert the raise to a growth factor: 1 + 8/100 = 1.08. Your salary is multiplied by 1.08.
      2. Convert inflation to a growth factor: 1 + 5/100 = 1.05. Prices are multiplied by 1.05.
      3. Divide the two factors: 1.08 / 1.05 = 1.0285714... This ratio is how much more your salary buys than before.
      4. Subtract one and convert back to percent: (1.0285714 − 1) × 100 = 2.857142857142857%. That is the real raise.

      The calculator above performs these steps with full floating-point precision and displays each intermediate value, so you can verify the arithmetic yourself. Nothing is rounded inside the formula — rounding happens only in the display.

      Why You Cannot Just Subtract

      A tempting shortcut is to subtract: 8% raise minus 5% inflation equals a 3% real raise. The correct answer is 2.857%. The shortcut overstates the result, and the error grows as the numbers get larger. The reason is that percentages compound on different bases — the raise applies to your salary, inflation applies to prices — so the relationship is a ratio, not a difference.

      For small numbers the shortcut is close enough for conversation (2.86 vs 3.00), which is why the myth survives. But precision matters when the figures are large or when you are comparing offers across years: at 12% inflation and a 15% raise, subtraction says +3.00% while the formula says +2.68% — a gap of nearly a third of a percentage point. The calculator uses the ratio form always, so its answers are exact for any inputs.

      The Five-Year View: Compounding Cuts Both Ways

      One year of lagging inflation is a footnote; five years of it is a lifestyle change. Small annual gaps compound: a salary growing at 3% while prices grow at 5% loses about 1.9% of purchasing power each year, and after five years the cumulative loss exceeds 9%. Conversely, beating inflation by two points a year compounds into a double-digit real gain over the same span.

      The projection table above shows this mechanically: each year's salary grows by the nominal rate, and each year's "value in today's money" divides by cumulative inflation. It assumes constant rates, which never happens in reality — its purpose is to illustrate the shape of compounding, not to predict your finances. Run it with your own numbers and watch how a one-point change in either rate bends the five-year curve.

      Reading Your Result

      The calculator labels the outcome in plain descriptive terms:

      • Beating inflation (above +0.5%): purchasing power grew. The raise more than covered rising prices.
      • Roughly in line (−0.5% to +0.5%): the raise essentially matched inflation. Dollars changed; buying power did not.
      • Losing to inflation (below −0.5%): purchasing power shrank. More dollars, less buying power.

      These are mathematical descriptions of the numbers you entered, not recommendations. Whether a given real raise is "good" depends on your industry, role, performance, local labor market, and total compensation — factors no formula can see.       Common Misunderstandings

      • "A 5% raise is always good." Against 2% inflation it is excellent (+2.94% real). Against 7% inflation it is a pay cut (−1.87% real). The raise cannot be judged without the inflation number beside it.
      • "No raise means I stayed the same." With 4% inflation, a frozen salary is a −3.85% real change. Standing still nominally means moving backward in real terms.
      • "Bonuses fix it." A one-time bonus raises one year's income but does not compound into the salary base. A smaller permanent raise usually overtakes a larger one-time bonus within a couple of years — compare them with the projection table.
      • "Inflation affects everyone equally." Headline inflation is an average over a basket of goods. If your spending skews toward fast-rising categories (rent, childcare, healthcare), your personal inflation rate can differ substantially from the published figure.

      Where the Inflation Number Comes From

      The inflation inputs people typically use come from consumer price indices — the Consumer Price Index (CPI) in the US, the CPIH in the UK, or equivalents elsewhere. These track the price of a representative basket of household goods and services. When commentators say "inflation was 5%," they usually mean the index rose 5% over twelve months.

      Two caveats keep the math honest. First, the basket is an average household's, not yours — the misunderstandings section above explains why your personal rate may differ. Second, timing matters: compare your raise against inflation over the same period the raise covers. An annual raise pairs with twelve-month inflation; a mid-year adjustment pairs with the inflation since your last change. The COLA tracker above lets you log year-by-year pairs so the comparison stays apples-to-apples.

      Frequently asked questions

      What is the difference between a nominal raise and a real raise?

      The nominal raise is the percentage increase in your paycheck dollars. The real raise adjusts for inflation — it measures the change in what those dollars can buy. If you get an 8% raise while prices rise 5%, your real raise is about 2.86%, computed as ((1.08/1.05)-1)*100.

      Why can't I just subtract inflation from my raise?

      Because percentages compound on different bases — the raise applies to your salary, inflation to prices — so the correct relationship is a ratio, not a difference. Subtraction (8-5=3) overstates the true answer (2.857%). The gap is small for low numbers but grows as inflation rises.

      What does a negative real raise mean?

      It means your purchasing power shrank: prices rose faster than your pay. A 3% raise during 6% inflation is a -2.83% real raise — you earn more dollars but can afford less than before. This is a mathematical description, not financial advice.

      Is a 0% real raise bad?

      Mathematically it means your buying power is unchanged — your pay exactly kept pace with prices. Whether that is satisfactory depends on your role, performance, market, and total compensation, which no formula can assess.

      How does the ask-amount planner work?

      It inverts the formula: given your target real raise and an inflation rate, it computes the nominal raise required as ((1+target/100)*(1+inflation/100)-1)*100. For example, targeting 3% real with 5% inflation requires about an 8.15% nominal raise. It is arithmetic, not a recommendation.

      Does this tool give financial advice?

      No. It is an educational calculator that demonstrates the inflation-adjustment formula with full step-by-step working. It makes no personalized claims or recommendations. For compensation or financial decisions, consider your full situation and, where appropriate, a qualified professional.
