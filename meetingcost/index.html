Why Meetings Feel Free (But Aren't)

Meetings are the only business expense with no receipt. Nobody approves a $346 purchase without a second thought, yet a one-hour meeting with eight people earning $90,000 a year costs exactly that — and it happens five times a day without anyone signing anything. Salary is the largest line item in most companies, and meetings are where huge chunks of it quietly evaporate. The first step to fixing it is making the cost visible, which is exactly what the ticker above does.

The psychology is simple: when a cost is invisible, it is treated as zero. A manager who would never approve a $346 team lunch without a reason will happily call a $346 meeting on a hunch. Once the meter is running on the wall — or even just in everyone's head — the conversation changes from "let's have a meeting about it" to "is this worth $346?" Most of the time, the honest answer reshapes the meeting: fewer people, half the time, or an email instead.

The Math Behind the Ticker

The calculator uses the standard cost-accounting shortcut: divide annual salary by 2,080 working hours (40 hours times 52 weeks) to get an hourly cost, then multiply by attendees and duration. An employee earning $90,000 costs $43.27 per hour by this math, or about 72 cents per minute. Eight of them in a room burn $5.77 every minute — $346 per hour.

That is actually the conservative number. The fully-loaded cost of an employee — salary plus benefits, payroll taxes, office space, equipment, and HR overhead — typically runs 1.25 to 1.4 times base salary. At a 1.3 multiplier, that $346 meeting really costs about $450. The calculator shows the base-salary figure so the math stays transparent; if you want the loaded number, bump the average salary input by 30% and watch the ticker jump.

What the Big Number Includes (and Misses)

The ticker counts the salaries of the people in the room for the minutes the meeting runs. What it does not count is often bigger: preparation time, the context-switching tax (it takes roughly 20 minutes to get back into deep work after an interruption), and the opportunity cost of what those people would otherwise have produced. Researchers who study this estimate that the true cost of a meeting can be two to three times the salary math once you include recovery time.

On the other hand, not every attendee minute is equal. A decision that unblocks a ten-person team for a week is worth far more than the hour it took. The ticker is not an argument that all meetings are waste — it is a way to weigh the cost side of the scale honestly so the value side has to justify itself.

The Yearly Burn Changes Everything

The most shocking number on this page is not the per-meeting cost — it is the yearly burn. A single weekly one-hour meeting with eight $90k earners costs about $18,000 a year in salary alone. Five such meetings a week, the default above, burns roughly $90,000 a year: an entire full-time salary, spent sitting in rooms. When executives see that number attached to a recurring meeting with no clear owner or outcome, the meeting tends to get shorter, smaller, or cancelled within days.

This is also why recurring meetings deserve more scrutiny than one-offs. A one-hour meeting that happens once costs $346. The same meeting on a weekly invite costs $18,000 a year, and most recurring meetings outlive their purpose by months. Put an expiry date on every recurring meeting — "we'll revisit whether this is still needed in six weeks" — and the zombie meetings die on their own.

When a Meeting Is Worth It

Use a simple test before calling one: what decision will be made, and what does a faster or better decision earn? If a $346 meeting resolves a question that unblocks $5,000 of engineering time, it is a bargain. If the outcome is "let's discuss further next week," it was expensive theater. Write the decision at the top of the agenda — literally, "Decision: choose a launch date" — and end the meeting the moment it is made. The ticker rewards short meetings twice: fewer minutes, and sharper thinking.

A second test: could this be asynchronous? Status updates, FYIs, and anything where no real-time debate is needed belong in a document or a short recorded video, which people consume at 1.5x speed on their own schedule. Reserve synchronous time — the most expensive format — for disagreement, brainstorming, and decisions that genuinely need back-and-forth.

Cutting the Cost Without Killing Collaboration

Start with the attendee list, because cost scales linearly with heads. Every meeting has a core of two to four people who must be there and a halo of people invited "just in case." Halve the halo and you halve the cost with zero loss of output — send the others notes. Next, halve the default duration: most 60-minute meetings fit in 30 minutes once an agenda exists, and most 30-minute meetings fit in 15.

Then fix the cadence. Daily standups that should be twice-weekly, weekly syncs that should be biweekly — each halving of frequency halves the yearly burn. Finally, protect maker time: cluster meetings into one or two blocks per day so the remaining hours stay contiguous for real work. The ticker above makes each of these cuts tangible: change the inputs and watch thousands of dollars reappear.

The Live Timer Trick

There is a reason this calculator has a live timer: social accountability. Teams that display a running cost during the meeting consistently finish early. Nobody wants to be the person who dragged a $400 conversation into a $600 one for no reason. Try it once in your most expensive recurring meeting — project the ticker, start the timer, and watch how quickly "quick question" gets quicker.

One warning: do not use the ticker to shame individuals. The point is to question the meeting, not the attendees. Frame it as "let's make sure this was worth it" rather than "look what you cost." Used well, it becomes a shared joke that quietly kills bad meetings. Used badly, it becomes surveillance, and people will hate it — rightly.

Common Mistakes in Meeting Math

The biggest mistake is counting only the meeting itself. Add preparation: if four people each spent 30 minutes preparing, that is two more person-hours at the same burn rate. The second mistake is ignoring the recovery tax — a 30-minute meeting in the middle of the morning can easily destroy two hours of deep work across the team. The third is treating all salaries as equal: a meeting of six junior staff and one VP is dominated by the VP's time, so think about whose minutes are really being spent.

Finally, do not forget inflation of a different kind: meeting creep. The 30-minute sync becomes 45, then 60; the attendee list grows by one each month. Re-run the numbers quarterly for your recurring meetings. The ticker does not lie, and neither does the calendar.

Making It Stick: Team Norms That Survive

Individual awareness fades; team norms endure. The highest-leverage norms cost nothing to adopt. Default to 25 and 50 minutes instead of 30 and 60 — calendar apps allow it, and the shorter default quietly reclaims hundreds of hours a year. Require an agenda for any meeting over 15 minutes: no agenda, no meeting, no exceptions — the rule kills more bad meetings than any lecture about efficiency. Declare one no-meeting day per week so makers get at least one contiguous deep-work day; protect it fiercely for a month and watch output rise. End with owners and deadlines: every decision gets a name and a date, read aloud in the last two minutes. Finally, audit quarterly: pull up the recurring meetings, re-run them through the calculator above, and cancel the ones that cannot justify their yearly burn. Teams that do this typically cut meeting load by a third within two quarters — not through heroics, but because the ticker made the cost impossible to unsee.

Frequently asked questions

How do you calculate the cost of a meeting?

Divide each attendee's annual salary by 2,080 working hours to get their hourly cost, multiply by the number of attendees, then by the meeting length in hours. An 8-person, one-hour meeting at a $90,000 average salary costs about $346. This calculator does that math live, including a per-minute burn rate.

Should I include benefits in the salary number?

For the fully-loaded cost, yes — add roughly 25 to 40 percent to the salary figure to cover benefits, payroll taxes, and overhead. The calculator uses base salary by default so the math stays transparent; bump the average-salary input by 30% any time you want the loaded number.

What is the 2,080 hours number?

It is the standard full-time work year: 40 hours per week times 52 weeks. Dividing salary by 2,080 gives the hourly cost of an employee's time. It ignores vacation and holidays, which makes it a slightly conservative estimate — the real hourly cost is a touch higher.

How much does a recurring weekly meeting cost per year?

Multiply one meeting's cost by 52. A weekly one-hour meeting with eight people at $90k salaries costs about $346 per session, or roughly $18,000 per year. Five such meetings a week burns around $90,000 a year — an entire salary spent in conference rooms.

Does the calculator count preparation and context switching?

No — the ticker counts salary cost for the minutes people are in the meeting. Preparation time and the 15 to 20 minutes it takes to refocus afterward are real but harder to measure, so treat the ticker as a floor: the true cost is usually 1.5 to 2 times higher.

What is a good rule for deciding if a meeting is worth it?

Name the decision the meeting must produce before it starts. If the meeting resolves something worth clearly more than its ticker cost — unblocking a team, avoiding a costly mistake — hold it. If the outcome is just another discussion, make it an email and keep the money.
