What a Cap Rate Actually Tells You

The capitalization rate — cap rate — is the real estate industry's shorthand for yield. It answers one clean question: if I bought this property with cash and held it for a year, what percentage of the price would come back to me as operating profit? The formula is disarmingly simple: net operating income divided by purchase price. A $250,000 rental producing $13,592 of net operating income has a cap rate of 5.44%. That is it. No financing, no taxes, no appreciation — just the property's raw earning power expressed as a percentage.

That simplicity is both the metric's power and its trap. Because every investor uses the same definition, cap rates are the common language of deal comparison: a 7% cap deal in Cleveland and a 4.5% cap deal in Austin can be discussed in the same sentence. But the number says nothing about why the yield differs — risk, growth, taxes, and management headache all hide behind that single percentage. This guide walks through building an honest cap rate, reading it against the market, and knowing exactly where the metric stops being useful.

Building Net Operating Income Line by Line

Cap rate math is only as honest as the NOI beneath it, and NOI is where most back-of-the-napkin analyses quietly lie. The correct sequence: start with gross scheduled rent (monthly rent times twelve), subtract a vacancy and collection allowance (5 to 10 percent for most residential markets — use the property's actual trailing vacancy if higher), then subtract operating expenses. Operating expenses include property taxes, insurance, maintenance and repairs, property management fees, HOA dues, utilities you pay as the owner, landscaping, and pest control.

Two things are deliberately excluded. Debt service is not an operating expense — the mortgage is a financing choice, not a property characteristic, and including it would make the same building show a different cap rate for every buyer. Income taxes and depreciation are excluded too, since they depend on the owner's tax situation. Capital expenditures (a new roof, HVAC replacement) sit in a gray zone: many investors subtract a capex reserve from NOI for an "economic" cap rate, which is more conservative and often more truthful for older buildings.

The Expense Lines Investors Forget

Ask a new landlord for their expenses and you will hear taxes, insurance, and maybe maintenance. The calculator above forces the full ledger because the forgotten lines are where deals die. Property management — 8 to 10 percent of gross rent — must be included even if you self-manage today; your time has a cost, and the next buyer will price it. Maintenance deserves 1 to 2 percent of property value per year, more for older buildings. Vacancy deserves its own line rather than wishful thinking: a property that sat empty two months last year has a real vacancy rate, not the 0 percent in the seller's pro forma.

Then there are the quiet killers: rising insurance premiums (up sharply in many states), special assessments from HOAs, water and sewer bills in tenant-friendly jurisdictions where you cannot separately meter, and turnover costs — paint, cleaning, and leasing fees every time a tenant leaves. A good rule: if your total operating expenses land below 35 percent of gross rent on an older property, you are probably missing something. The 50 percent rule — that expenses consume roughly half of gross rent over time — exists because generations of landlords kept discovering the same forgotten lines you are discovering now.

What Counts as a Good Cap Rate?

"Good" is local. As a rough national frame: single-family rentals often trade at 5 to 8 percent, Sunbelt multifamily near 5 percent, coastal gateway markets (New York, San Francisco, Boston) at 3.5 to 4.5 percent, and value-add or tertiary-market deals at 8 percent and up. The pattern is not random — cap rates are prices of risk and growth. A 4 percent cap in a growing coastal market reflects investors accepting low current yield for strong rent growth and appreciation. A 9 percent cap in a shrinking market reflects the opposite: high yield demanded as compensation for stagnation and risk.

This is why cap rate compression matters. When investors flood into a market, prices rise faster than rents, and cap rates fall — the market is "paying up" for that location. When capital flees, cap rates expand. Comparing your deal's cap rate to the local average for the same property type (as the comparison bar above does) tells you whether you are buying yield at a discount or paying a premium — and forces the question of whether the premium is justified by growth.

Cap Rate vs Cash-on-Cash: The Financing Question

Here is where beginners get burned. The cap rate pretends you paid all cash. But you probably did not — and leverage changes everything. Cash-on-cash return divides your actual annual cash flow (NOI minus mortgage payments) by the actual cash you invested (down payment plus closing costs). In the example above, a 5.44% cap rate becomes a negative cash-on-cash return at 25% down and 7% interest, because the debt service exceeds the NOI.

The relationship follows one rule: if your loan's interest rate is below the cap rate, leverage boosts your cash-on-cash return; if it is above, leverage drags it down (this is positive vs negative leverage). At 7% rates, many 5%-cap deals show negative leverage — the property is fine, the financing is expensive. Neither metric is "right"; they answer different questions. Cap rate judges the property. Cash-on-cash judges your specific deal structure. Underwrite with both, and never let a seller's cap rate distract you from your cash-on-cash reality.

How Investors Raise a Property's Cap Rate

You cannot change the market's cap rate, but you can change a property's NOI — and since price is fixed at purchase, NOI growth flows straight into yield. The playbook: raise rents to market on turnover (the single biggest lever on under-rented buildings), cut controllable expenses (re-bid insurance, submeter water, challenge the tax assessment), add income streams (storage, parking, laundry, pet rent), and reduce vacancy through faster turns and better tenant screening. A $200 monthly rent increase on a $250,000 property lifts the cap rate by nearly a full percentage point — that is the math behind every value-add business plan.

Be honest about which levers are real. "Pro forma" cap rates based on rents the seller never achieved are marketing, not math. Underwrite on trailing-twelve-month income, then model your improvements separately. If the deal only works at the pro forma cap rate, you are not buying yield — you are buying a construction project with a yield story attached.

Where Cap Rate Misleads

Cap rate is a single-year snapshot, and real estate is a multi-decade movie. It ignores rent growth (a 4.5% cap with 4% annual rent growth beats a 6% cap with flat rents within a few years), appreciation, tax benefits like depreciation, principal paydown on your mortgage (a real return the cap rate never sees), and capex timing (two identical cap rates can hide very different roof ages). It also assumes stable occupancy — one bad eviction or a major repair blows up any single year's number.

Serious investors therefore pair cap rate with IRR (internal rate of return over a hold period, capturing growth and sale) and equity multiple (total cash returned per dollar invested). Think of cap rate as the screening metric — it tells you which deals deserve a deeper look — and IRR as the decision metric. A deal can have a mediocre cap rate and a great IRR (growth market, value-add plan) or a great cap rate and a terrible IRR (declining market, deferred maintenance). The percentage is the start of the analysis, never the end.

Putting It All Together

The disciplined workflow: build NOI from trailing actuals with every expense line represented, divide by the all-in purchase price, and compare the result to same-market, same-type deals. Then layer in your financing to see cash-on-cash, and sanity-check the story — does this yield make sense for this location, this building, this business plan? Run the numbers above with conservative inputs first. If the deal still clears your bar with honest vacancy and full expenses, you have something worth pursuing. If it only works with 0% vacancy and no management fee, the calculator just saved you a quarter of a million dollars.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good cap rate for a rental property?

Single-family rentals often trade at 5–8%, Sunbelt multifamily near 5%, coastal gateway markets at 3.5–4.5%, and value-add deals at 8%+. Higher cap rates usually signal higher risk or lower growth expectations, not automatically a better investment.

How do you calculate cap rate?

Divide net operating income (NOI) by the purchase price and multiply by 100. NOI is annual rent minus vacancy allowance and operating expenses — taxes, insurance, maintenance, management, HOA — but before mortgage payments and income taxes.

What is the difference between cap rate and cash-on-cash return?

Cap rate measures the property's yield as if you paid all cash, ignoring financing. Cash-on-cash divides your actual annual cash flow (after mortgage payments) by the cash you actually invested. Leverage can push cash-on-cash above or below the cap rate.

Does cap rate include the mortgage payment?

No — NOI excludes debt service on purpose, so cap rates stay comparable across buyers with different financing. To see the effect of your loan, use cash-on-cash return instead.

Should vacancy be included in the cap rate calculation?

Yes. Always subtract a vacancy and collection allowance (typically 5–10%) from gross rent before computing NOI. Ignoring vacancy is the most common way investors overstate a cap rate.

Can I compare cap rates across different cities?

Only cautiously. Cap rates embed local risk, growth expectations, taxes and regulations. Compare within the same market and property type first; a lower cap rate in a growing market can beat a higher one in a shrinking market over time.
