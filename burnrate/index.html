What Is Startup Runway?

Startup runway is the number of months your company can keep operating before its cash balance hits zero, assuming nothing changes. It is the single most important number for an early-stage founder: it sets the deadline for becoming profitable, closing a funding round, or cutting costs. If you have $1.5 million in the bank and you spend a net $100,000 every month, your runway is 15 months. Simple — until reality intervenes with hires, raises, server bills, and the occasional surprise tax payment. That is why a static division problem is not enough, and why founders reach for a startup runway calculator that models the future month by month.

Runway thinking changes how you run the company. A founder with 20 months of runway can hire deliberately, negotiate from strength, and walk away from a bad term sheet. A founder with 4 months of runway is negotiating with a gun to their head — and investors can smell it. Knowing your exact zero-cash date, rather than a vague "sometime next year," is what separates a plan from a hope.

How to Calculate Burn Rate

Burn rate is the amount of cash your company spends each month, and a burn rate calculator starts by separating two concepts. Gross burn is everything going out the door: salaries, rent, software, ads, contractors — the full monthly spend. Net burn is gross burn minus monthly revenue: the amount by which your bank balance actually shrinks each month. If you spend $120,000 and bring in $25,000, your gross burn is $120,000 and your net burn is $95,000.

The classic runway formula is: runway (months) = cash in bank ÷ net monthly burn. So $1,500,000 ÷ $95,000 ≈ 15.8 months. This works as a first approximation, but it assumes burn and revenue stay perfectly flat — they never do. Revenue (hopefully) grows, salaries step up with each hire, and one-off expenses punch holes in the line. A proper startup cash runway model recomputes the balance every month, applying each future event to the month it lands in.

Gross Burn vs Net Burn: Which One Matters?

Both, for different reasons. Net burn drives the zero-cash date, so it is the number behind your runway. But gross burn tells you how exposed you are if revenue stumbles. A company with $200,000 gross burn and $180,000 revenue has a comfortable 2-month… no — it has a tiny $20,000 net burn and years of runway on paper. Yet if a single enterprise customer churns, the runway collapses overnight. Founders with high gross burn and high revenue are flying with a thinner margin of error than the net number suggests. Track both, and be honest about which revenue is truly recurring.

What Is a Good Burn Multiple?

The burn multiple — coined by David Sacks — measures how much cash you burn to generate each dollar of net new ARR: annual net burn ÷ net new ARR. Burn $1.2 million a year while adding $2 million of new ARR and your multiple is 0.6: elite. Spend $3 million to add $1 million and your multiple is 3: a warning light.

As a rough guide: under 1 is elite, 1–2 is healthy and exactly what Series A and B investors want to see, 2–3 means watch it (acceptable only while growth is genuinely fast), and over 3 is the danger zone where you must either cut burn or prove growth is about to inflect. The multiple is meaningless if net new ARR is zero or negative — with flat revenue, runway is the only number that matters. Note the subtlety founders miss: a great burn multiple with 4 months of runway still kills you. Efficiency buys you investor love; runway buys you time. You need both.

How Many Months of Runway Should a Startup Have?

The conventional wisdom is 18–24 months after a fundraise: roughly 12 months to execute and hit milestones, plus 6 months to run a fundraising process. In practice, the right number depends on your stage. Pre-seed companies often operate on 12–18 months and raise on momentum. Seed-stage companies should target 18–24 months because enterprise sales cycles and hiring plans need the buffer. Anything under 12 months means fundraising should already be on the calendar; under 6 months is a five-alarm fire that demands immediate cost cuts alongside the raise.

There is also a psychological dimension. Teams that know the cash position make calmer decisions. Founders who hide the runway number get rumor-driven anxiety instead. Share the number, share the plan to extend it, and update it monthly — it takes ten minutes with a calculator like this one.

When Should You Start Fundraising?

A venture round takes roughly 6 months from first pitch to money in the bank: 4–8 weeks of meetings, 3–4 weeks of diligence and term-sheet negotiation, and 4–8 weeks for legal close and wire. Count backward from your zero-cash date. If cash runs out in month 14, you should be pitching by month 8 — while you still have 8 months of cash and maximum negotiating leverage.

The most common fundraising mistake is starting at month 11 of a 12-month runway. You arrive desperate, investors sense it, and the process drags past your zero-cash date into a bridge round on punishing terms. Set the alert early: when your model says "start fundraising by" a date six months before zero cash, put it on the calendar and treat it as immovable.

How to Extend Your Runway Without Raising

Before you cut, model it. The fastest levers: pause non-critical hires (each hire is a permanent step up in the burn line), renegotiate vendor contracts and annualize software spend, shift contractors to milestone-based pay, and pull forward revenue with annual prepay discounts. A 25% burn cut on a $100,000 net burn extends a 10-month runway to over 13 months — often the difference between a rushed bridge and a proper Series A.

The slower, more powerful lever is revenue. Adding $20,000 of monthly recurring revenue has the same runway effect as cutting $20,000 of burn, but it also improves your burn multiple and your valuation. That is why the "cut" scenario in a good model trims burn slightly and assumes a small revenue dip — honesty about trade-offs beats fantasy math.

Scenario Planning: Base, Stretch, Cut

Never run a company on a single forecast. The base case is your current plan: today's burn, today's revenue, the hires you have actually approved. The stretch case assumes you hire faster and grow faster — burn up 20%, revenue up 30% — and shows whether the extra speed is worth the shorter runway. The cut case is lean mode: burn down 25%, revenue slightly down, maximum survival time.

The discipline is in the comparison. If stretch shortens runway from 15 months to 9 but triples the chance of hitting the milestone that unlocks your Series A, it may be the right call — made with open eyes. If cut extends you from 8 months to 13, it buys a real fundraise instead of a fire sale. Run all three every month; the future arrives faster than your spreadsheet.

Common Runway Mistakes Founders Make

First, forgetting one-off expenses: security audits, annual insurance, that $40,000 conference sponsorship. They do not show up in "monthly burn" but they absolutely show up in the bank balance. Second, modeling revenue as a smooth line — enterprise deals slip quarters, and consumer growth stalls. Third, ignoring hiring lag: the engineer you "plan to hire in March" usually starts in May, but their salary hits the burn line the day they sign. Fourth, confusing cash with runway after a fundraise — a $3 million round at $250,000 net burn is 12 months, not "we're set for two years." And fifth, the deadliest: not recomputing. A runway number from January is fiction by April. Recompute monthly, or better, keep a live model you actually open.

Frequently asked questions

How do you calculate startup runway?

Divide your current cash balance by your net monthly burn (total monthly spending minus monthly revenue). For example, $1,500,000 in the bank with a net burn of $95,000 per month gives about 15.8 months of runway. For an accurate answer, model each future month separately so hires, raises, one-off expenses and revenue changes bend the projection — which is exactly what BurnRate's month-by-month calculator does.

What is a good burn multiple for a startup?

Burn multiple is annual net burn divided by net new ARR. Under 1 is elite, 1–2 is healthy and what most Series A/B investors expect, 2–3 is a watch zone that is only acceptable while growth is very fast, and over 3 is dangerous. If your net new ARR is zero or negative, the multiple is meaningless and runway becomes the only metric that matters.

How many months of runway should a startup have?

After a fundraise, aim for 18–24 months: about 12 months to execute and hit milestones, plus roughly 6 months to run your next fundraising process. Below 12 months, fundraising should already be scheduled. Below 6 months, cut costs immediately and start fundraising this week — you have no buffer left.

When should I start fundraising before cash runs out?

Start about 6 months before your projected zero-cash date, because a venture round typically takes 6 months from first pitch to money in the bank. If your model says cash hits zero in month 14, begin pitching by month 8. Starting later destroys your negotiating leverage and risks the process dragging past your zero-cash date.

What is the difference between gross burn and net burn?

Gross burn is everything you spend each month — salaries, rent, tools, ads. Net burn is gross burn minus monthly revenue: the amount your bank balance actually shrinks by. Net burn determines your zero-cash date, but gross burn shows your exposure if revenue drops, so track both.

How can I extend my runway without raising money?

Cut net burn or grow revenue. The fastest levers are pausing non-critical hires, renegotiating vendor contracts, and annualizing software spend. A 25% burn cut can turn 10 months of runway into 13+. Growing monthly recurring revenue works even better: each new dollar of MRR extends runway exactly like a dollar of cost cuts, while also improving your burn multiple and valuation.
