The Sticker Price Is Not the Price

A $32,000 car with $4,000 down at 5.9 percent APR for 60 months costs $540 a month — and $4,401 in interest, for a total of $36,401. Most buyers negotiate the sticker price for hours and accept the financing terms in minutes, which is exactly backwards: on a typical loan, the financing decisions (rate and term) move the total cost by thousands, often more than the discount they fought for on the price. The monthly payment is the number dealers want you to focus on, because it is the easiest number to manipulate — stretch the term and any payment becomes "affordable."

The honest way to buy a car is total-cost-first. Compute the full amount you will pay — down payment plus every monthly payment — for each term you are considering, and choose the shortest term whose payment fits your budget comfortably. The calculator above does this live, including the term comparison table that shows what each extra year really costs.

How the Monthly Payment Is Calculated

Car loans use standard amortization: each month you pay interest on the remaining balance plus a slice of principal, so the payment stays constant while the interest portion shrinks. The formula takes the amount financed (price minus down payment minus trade-in), the monthly interest rate (APR divided by 12), and the number of months, and solves for the fixed payment. Two inputs dominate the result: the rate and the term. A single percentage point of APR on a $28,000, 60-month loan changes the payment by about $13 a month — $780 over the life of the loan. The term matters even more, as the next section shows.

The Term Trap: 72 Months of "Affordable"

Stretching from 60 to 72 months on that $28,000 loan at 5.9 percent drops the payment from $540 to about $463 — tempting — but adds roughly $1,000 in extra interest and keeps you in debt a full extra year. Worse, long terms create the underwater trap: cars depreciate fastest in the first two years, so with a 72- or 84-month loan you can owe more than the car is worth for years. If the car is totaled or you need to sell, you write a check for the difference — unless you bought gap insurance, which is itself a cost of the long term.

The rule of thumb from consumer advocates: keep the term at 60 months or less, and keep the total car payment under 15 percent of take-home pay. If the car you want needs 84 months to fit the budget, it does not fit the budget — buy a cheaper car, not a longer loan.

Down Payments and Trade-Ins: Your Two Levers

Every dollar of down payment or trade-in value is a dollar you never pay interest on. A $4,000 down payment on a 60-month loan at 5.9 percent saves about $630 in interest versus financing the full price — and it shrinks the underwater window at the start of the loan. Aim for at least 20 percent down on a new car and 10 percent on used; that roughly matches first-year depreciation, so you start the loan right-side up.

For trade-ins, know your car's value before you visit the dealer — check two or three pricing guides and get a written offer from an online buyer. Dealers love to blur the trade-in value into the new-car discount ("we'll give you $2,000 more for your trade!") while quietly removing $2,000 from the discount. Negotiate the new-car price and the trade-in value as separate numbers, and the calculator treats them as separate inputs for exactly that reason.

APR: Where the Real Negotiation Happens

Dealer financing is a profit center: the dealer marks up the lender's "buy rate" and keeps the difference. That is why "we got you 7.9 percent" deserves the same skepticism as the sticker price. Before you shop, get pre-approved by your bank or a credit union — that rate is your ceiling, and it turns the finance office conversation from a sales pitch into a competition. If the dealer beats your pre-approval, take it; if not, use your own loan.

Your credit score drives the rate more than your haggling does. The difference between top-tier and mid-tier credit can be two full percentage points — over $1,500 on a typical loan. Check your score a few months before buying; paying down a card balance or two can move you into a better tier. And watch for the oldest trick: the "spot delivery" where you drive home and get called back days later because "the financing fell through" at a higher rate. Never take the car until the financing is final and in writing.

New vs Used: The Depreciation Math

A new car loses roughly 20 percent of its value in the first year and about 50 percent in five. That makes a two- to three-year-old car the value sweet spot: most of the depreciation is done, the car is still modern and often under factory warranty, and the loan is smaller. Run the calculator for both scenarios — a $32,000 new car versus a $22,000 two-year-old version of the same model — and compare total costs, not just payments. The used car's lower price usually wins by $8,000 to $12,000 all-in, which is why financial planners keep recommending it despite the undeniable smell of a new car.

Total Cost of Ownership Goes Beyond the Loan

The loan is only part of what the car costs. Insurance (get quotes before you buy — the same driver can pay double for different models), fuel or charging, maintenance, tires, and registration add thousands per year. A useful sanity check: the 20/4/10 rule — 20 percent down, a loan of no more than 4 years, and total monthly car expenses under 10 percent of gross income. If the car you want breaks the rule, the rule is telling you something honest.

Before You Sign

Run this final checklist in the finance office: confirm the APR, term, and amount financed match what you agreed (compare against this calculator on your phone); decline add-ons you did not ask for — extended warranties, paint protection, and VIN etching are high-margin extras you can almost always buy cheaper elsewhere or skip; verify there is no prepayment penalty; and never sign a contract with blank fields. The deal will still be there tomorrow. The pressure to sign tonight is manufactured, and the math you did at home does not change under fluorescent lights.

Refinancing: Your Second Chance at a Better Rate

The rate you sign is not the rate you are stuck with. If your credit score has improved since you bought the car — or if market rates have dropped — refinancing can cut your APR by a point or more with minimal hassle. The math is the same as the purchase: run your remaining balance, the new rate, and the remaining term through this calculator and compare total interest both ways. Two rules keep refinancing smart: never extend the term just to lower the payment (that is the term trap in reverse — you will pay more interest for the illusion of savings), and watch for prepayment penalties on the old loan and origination fees on the new one, which eat the savings. The sweet spot is refinancing within the first year or two, when the balance is high and rate improvements have the most dollars to work on. Credit unions consistently offer the best refinance rates, and many let you check your rate without a hard credit pull. Set a calendar reminder for twelve months after purchase — future you might be driving the same car for $40 less a month.

Frequently asked questions

How is a car loan monthly payment calculated?

With standard amortization: the amount financed (price minus down payment minus trade-in) is repaid in equal monthly installments covering interest on the remaining balance plus principal. The payment depends on the amount financed, the APR divided by 12, and the number of months. The calculator above runs this exact formula.

Is a 72-month car loan a bad idea?

Usually. Compared with 60 months, a 72-month term lowers the payment but adds roughly $1,000+ in interest on a typical loan, keeps you in debt a year longer, and leaves you owing more than the car is worth for years (underwater). Consumer advocates recommend 60 months or less.

How much should I put down on a car?

Aim for at least 20% down on a new car and 10% on used — that roughly matches first-year depreciation so you start the loan right-side up instead of underwater. Every dollar down is a dollar you never pay interest on.

Should I take dealer financing or my bank's loan?

Get pre-approved by your bank or credit union first — that rate is your ceiling. Dealers mark up the lender's buy rate for profit, so treat the finance office as a competition: if the dealer beats your pre-approval, take it; otherwise use your own loan.

What is a good APR for a car loan?

It depends on your credit score and the market, but as a rough guide top-tier credit earns rates a point or two above Treasury yields while mid-tier credit pays 2–4 points more. Check your score months before buying — moving up one credit tier can save over $1,500 on a typical loan.

New vs used — which costs less overall?

Almost always used. A new car loses ~20% of its value in year one and ~50% in five years, so a 2–3-year-old car gives you most of the life for far less money — typically $8,000–$12,000 less all-in than the same model new. Compare total costs in the calculator, not just monthly payments.
